Curious Minds · dunnart spleen

Spot the stranger

This is a very thin slice of a dunnart’s spleen, seen through a microscope. Dunnarts are small meat-eating marsupials — like a tiny Tasmanian devil! The pale round patches are follicles: little groups of white blood cells. The red-brown stain around their edges is what we’re here to look at.

What am I looking at?

  • The follicle A round group of white blood cells. They are part of the body’s immune system.
  • The red-brown stain A dye that only sticks to cells with ID checkers on them. Those cells sit around the outside of the follicle.
  • Everything else The rest of the spleen. It is paler because there is nothing there for the dye to stick to.

What does an ID checker do?

An ID checker works a bit like show and tell. A cell picks up a tiny piece of whatever is floating past — maybe a bit of germ, maybe a bit of the dunnart itself — and holds it up on the ID checker so other immune cells can have a look.

If the piece belongs to the dunnart, nothing happens. If it’s a stranger, the immune system gets to work clearing it out.

“Hang on — you’re a stranger!”

Why more shapes is better

Every ID checker is a slightly different shape, and each shape can only hold pieces that fit it. If an animal has lots of different shapes, it can spot lots of different strangers. If it only has a few shapes, some germs slip past without being noticed.

A family with only a few shapes

Dunnarts and Tasmanian devils belong to the same family: the meat-eating marsupials, called Dasyuridae. Compared with most mammals, this family has only a few ID checker shapes to share around.

Part of the reason is history. Only a small number of their ancestors ever reached Australia. Later, the devils on Tasmania were cut off from the mainland, and their numbers have dropped very low more than once. Every time a population gets small, some ID checker shapes are lost — and they don’t come back.

Why this matters for devils

Tasmanian devils are dying from a cancer that spreads from devil to devil. That is very unusual — cancer normally stays inside the animal it started in. But devils bite each other’s faces when they squabble over food, and living tumour cells get passed from one devil’s mouth into another’s wound.

Because devils have so few ID checker shapes, the tumour cells don’t look like strangers — they look like they belong. The immune system never spots them, so the cancer keeps growing.

The good news

An immune system can be taught to notice something new. Scientists in Tasmania are working on a vaccine that helps devils recognise the tumour cells, and trials are underway. The same idea could help protect other Australian animals too — maybe even using vaccines hidden in food they like to eat.

Words to know

Cancer
When some of the body’s own cells start growing out of control and form a lump called a tumour.
Dasyuridaedaz-ee-YOO-rid-ee
The family of meat-eating marsupials. Dunnarts, quolls and Tasmanian devils all belong to it.
Dunnart
A small meat-eating marsupial, about the size of a mouse. It comes out at night to hunt insects and spiders.
Follicle
A round group of white blood cells inside the spleen. These are the pale patches on the slide.
Germ
A tiny living thing, like a bacterium or a virus, that can make an animal sick.
ID checker
A tiny checker on the outside of a cell. It holds up tiny pieces of things so the immune system can check whether they belong.
Immune system
The parts of the body that find germs and other things that don’t belong, and get rid of them.
Marsupial
A mammal whose babies are born very tiny and finish growing in a pouch. Kangaroos, koalas, dunnarts and devils are all marsupials.
Spleen
An organ near the stomach. It filters the blood and is full of immune cells.
Vaccine
A safe preview of a germ or tumour, given to an animal so its immune system learns to recognise the real thing.
White blood cells
The cells of the immune system. Some check what is around them; others clear out things that don’t belong.
