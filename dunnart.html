Spot the stranger

A thin slice through the spleen of a dunnart — a small marsupial, and a close cousin of the Tasmanian devil. The pale round patches are follicles: crowds of white blood cells. The red-brown stain around their edges is what we came to look at.

What am I looking at?

  • The follicle A ball of white blood cells — the barracks, waiting to be told where to go.
  • The red-brown stain A dye that sticks only to MHC markers, sitting on cells around the outside of the follicle.
  • Everything else The rest of the spleen. Paler, because the dye has nothing to grab onto there.

What the markers do

An MHC marker is not a weapon. It is a display stand. A patrolling cell picks up a scrap of whatever is drifting past — bacteria, virus, a broken piece of cell — and holds it out on a marker for other immune cells to inspect.

Why variety wins

Every marker is a slightly different shape, and each one only grabs scraps that fit it. The more different shapes a population carries between them, the more kinds of stranger get noticed. Fewer shapes, more gaps.

Dasyuridae were dealt a small hand

Dunnarts and Tasmanian devils are both Dasyuridae, and between them they carry fewer MHC shapes than most mammals. Marsupials reached Australia from South America across Antarctica, and only some of them made the trip. Tasmania later cut its devils off as an island population. Numbers have crashed more than once since. Each squeeze is a population bottleneck, and variety lost in one does not come back.

The devil’s problem

Tasmanian devils are dying from a facial cancer that spreads between animals. That is extraordinarily rare — cancer is normally stuck in the body it started in. Devils bite each other's faces while feeding and mating, so living tumour cells pass straight from one mouth into the next animal's wound. And because devils carry so few different MHC shapes, those cells arrive looking like they belong. The alarm never goes off.

The good news

You can teach an immune system to see something it has been ignoring. Researchers in Tasmania have been immunising devils against the facial tumour, and trials are underway. The same thinking is being explored for protecting other Australian wildlife — including vaccines hidden in baits.
